# Playbook: AI-Powered SEO Content Production
**Author:** Nurul Hanifah  
**Based on:** Research from 10 SEO & AI practitioners  
**Last updated:** June 2026

---

## Introduction

This playbook is a practical guide for producing SEO content using AI tools, built from the insights of 10 active practitioners in the field. It is not a theory document. Every recommendation is grounded in what working SEO professionals are actually doing in 2025 and 2026.

As an SEO writer and content manager with 14 years of experience, I built this playbook to answer one question: how do we produce content that ranks in both traditional search and AI-powered search, without sacrificing quality?

---

## Part 1: Core Framework

Before producing any content, understand the three layers of modern search optimization:

| Layer | Full Name | What It Does |
|---|---|---|
| SEO | Search Engine Optimization | Gets you found by search engines |
| AEO | Answer Engine Optimization | Helps you become the direct answer |
| GEO | Generative Engine Optimization | Gets you cited and recommended by AI |

These three layers are sequential. You cannot skip SEO and expect GEO to work. As Nico (AI Ranking) explains, SEO accounts for approximately 70% of the effort and success. The remaining 30% covers AEO and GEO techniques.

*(Source: Nico | AI Ranking, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nOk-kX8FndI)*

## Part 2: Core Recommendations

The following recommendations are drawn from patterns that appeared consistently across multiple sources. Not every suggestion from every expert made it into this list. Only those supported by more than one perspective, or backed by sufficiently strong data, were included.

### 2.1 Stop using traffic as your main KPI

Traffic is no longer the right metric for AI-era SEO. Zero-click searches increased from 57% before AI mode to 59% after its rollout, and in full AI mode, an estimated 80 to 93% of searches result in zero clicks.

What to measure instead: citations, brand mentions in AI answers, share of voice in AI results, and impressions in Google Search Console's Generative AI report.

*(Source: Neil Patel, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3q3WBkSoANA; Nico | AI Ranking, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nOk-kX8FndI)*

### 2.2 Build topical authority through content clusters

Do not publish isolated articles. Build topic clusters: a money page supported by mid- and top-funnel content, all internally linked. Google rewards sites that comprehensively cover a topic over those with scattered individual pages.

Recommended structure:
- Bottom: Transactional money page (product, service, or pricing)
- Middle: Comparison and commercial content linking to the money page
- Top: Educational blog posts covering the full topic space

*(Source: Matt Kenyon / Surfer Academy, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7DRO4rEIHDk)*

### 2.3 Write content that AI can extract

Each section of your content should stand alone as a coherent answer if extracted by an AI system. H2 headings should pose a question, and the paragraph immediately after should provide a concise, direct answer. Avoid verbose introductions.

Bad: "In today's digital landscape, businesses seek ways to improve their online visibility..."
Good: "Technical SEO is the process of optimizing your website infrastructure so that search engines can crawl, index, and rank your pages effectively."

*(Source: Nico | AI Ranking, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nOk-kX8FndI)*

### 2.4 Convert SEO keywords into AI prompts at scale

Use Google Sheets' =AI or =Gemini function to convert traditional SEO keywords into natural-language queries that match how people ask AI assistants. This bridges the gap between traditional keyword research and AI search optimization.

*(Source: Lily Ray, https://www.linkedin.com/posts/lily-ray-44755615_heres-a-little-hack-ive-been-using-to-quickly-activity-7470166067043147776-6kTD, June 10, 2026)*

### 2.5 Traditional SEO rankings still drive AI citations

76% of AI overview citations come from pages ranking in the top 10 of traditional search results. This means foundational SEO, including quality content, clean structure, backlinks, and schema markup, is not dead. It is the prerequisite for AI visibility.

*(Source: Nico | AI Ranking, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nOk-kX8FndI; Cyrus Shepard, https://www.linkedin.com/posts/cyrusshepard_how-traditional-seo-rankings-multiply-ai-activity-7470898331137880064-6UUP)*

### 2.6 Build a brand knowledge base before producing AI content

Before using AI tools to produce content, upload your brand documents including FAQs, case studies, messaging guidelines, policies, and customer language into Claude Projects or ChatGPT Projects. This prevents hallucinations and ensures output is on-brand and accurate.

Key editorial rule: "If AI can easily create it, don't publish it." Add original data, first-hand experience, screenshots, and expert insights that AI cannot fabricate.

*(Source: Nathan Gotch, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mbCfRlY7elM)*

### 2.7 Monitor your content's appearance in AI Overviews via GSC

Google Search Console now provides a "Generative AI" report showing impressions for your pages in AI Overviews, AI Mode, and Discover. Use this data to identify which pages are being cited, then analyze what those pages offer that AI summaries do not: first-hand experience, original images, and deeper analysis.

*(Source: Marie Haynes, https://www.mariehaynes.com/new-aio-and-aimode-info-in-gsc/)*

### 2.8 Optimize for complex, nuanced queries

AI systems tend to answer simple queries directly without citations. Long-tail, complex queries of six or more words trigger AI overviews with citations at a rate of 77%, compared to only 23% for short queries. Focus content strategy on complex, specific topics where your expertise creates a genuine advantage.

*(Source: Neil Patel, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3q3WBkSoANA)*

### 2.9 Track four AI SEO metrics regularly

| Metric | What to Track |
|---|---|
| Market Weighted Coverage | Is your brand mentioned in AI answers across platforms? |
| Position | Where does your brand appear in AI answers? Aim for top 3. |
| Share of Voice | Your brand's dominance vs. competitors in AI results |
| Brand Coverage in Citations | Does your brand appear in sources AI cites? |

*(Source: Nathan Gotch, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mbCfRlY7elM)*

### 2.10 Understand how users behave differently in AI Mode vs AI Overviews

In AI Mode, 88% of users accept the AI's shortlist as-is, 74% pick the top result, and 64% click nothing. In AI Overviews, users compare, scroll, and return. Cursor coverage hits 83% of the visible page. These are two different optimization problems: AI Mode is a visibility problem at the model layer, while AI Overviews is a comparison problem on the SERP itself.

*(Source: Kevin Indig, https://www.linkedin.com/posts/kevinindig_the-same-google-user-behaves-differently-activity-7464612137668067328-sXZq)*

## Part 3: Where Experts Disagree

Not all experts in this research agree with one another. That is precisely where critical judgment becomes necessary. The following are three areas where I found significant differences in perspective, along with my own position after weighing both sides.

### Disagreement 1: Is traditional SEO dead or just evolving?

**Neil Patel argues:** Traditional SEO is effectively dead. Rankings and traffic no longer guarantee visibility. The new paradigm centers on building long-term recognized authority that AI systems can trust and cite. He frames this as a fundamental mindset shift, not an incremental update.

**Matt Kenyon (Surfer Academy) argues:** SEO is not dead. It is the foundation of AI search. Google drives 190x more traffic to websites than ChatGPT. AI assistants rely on traditional search indexes, so if your site is invisible to Google, it is invisible to AI. Doing SEO right is AI optimization.

**My take:** I side with Matt Kenyon. Neil Patel's framing is compelling for large brands but misleading for most content producers. The data supports Kenyon: 76% of AI overview citations come from top-10 ranked pages. Traditional SEO is still the entry point. What has changed is the output we optimize for, namely citations and brand mentions rather than clicks alone.

---

### Disagreement 2: How important is link building in the AI era?

**Nathan Gotch argues:** Brand mentions matter as much or more than traditional backlinks. AI platforms rely on brand presence in trusted retrieval sources. Even unlinked brand mentions can influence AI recommendations. The goal shifts from link authority to brand trust with AI systems.

**Matt Kenyon argues:** Backlinks remain a strong ranking signal and should not be abandoned. He recommends publishing original research, using journalist query platforms, and building linkable assets. The fundamentals of link building still apply.

**My take:** Both are right for different stages. For new content producers, Kenyon's traditional link building approach builds the necessary SEO foundation first. Gotch's brand mention strategy becomes more relevant once baseline authority exists. I would not abandon links in favor of mentions. I would pursue both in parallel.

---

### Disagreement 3: How much should AI be used in content production?

**Nathan Gotch argues:** AI is a core production tool but must be filtered by human expertise. His workflow uses AI to generate outlines and drafts from a knowledge base, then adds human review, original data, and real-world proof. The key rule: if AI can easily create it, do not publish it.

**Tom Capper raises a concern:** He references a test by Daniel Foley Carter where Claude SEO agents produced results described as "a whole lot of nonsense." AI can assist, but it cannot replace experienced editorial judgment.

**My take:** I agree with Gotch's workflow and validate Capper's concern at the same time. AI tools work well for drafting and structuring when guided by a strong knowledge base and a human editor with domain expertise. For someone with 14 years of SEO experience, AI is a drafting assistant, not a replacement. The risk is greatest for teams that use AI without that editorial filter in place.

## Part 4: What I Rejected and Why

Not everything recommended by the experts deserves to be followed without question. The following are two ideas I encountered in this research that I chose not to include in the playbook, along with clear reasoning for each decision.

### Rejected idea 1: Opting out of AI Overviews in Google Search Console

Marie Haynes mentions that Google now provides a toggle for website owners to opt out of having their content used in AI features. Some publishers have considered this on principle or because AI was misrepresenting their content.

**Why I rejected it:** For most content producers, opting out of AI Overviews is opting out of search visibility entirely. The smarter move is to stay opted in, monitor the new GSC Generative AI report, and use that data to improve content quality. Opting out removes your data and your opportunity to optimize.

---

### Rejected idea 2: Focusing exclusively on AI Mode optimization

Kevin Indig's research shows that in AI Mode, 88% of users accept the AI shortlist as-is and 64% click nothing. One interpretation of this data is to focus all energy on entering the AI Mode shortlist and ignore SERP optimization entirely.

**Why I rejected it:** The same data shows that AI Overviews users behave very differently. They compare, scroll, and evaluate options. These are higher-quality prospects. A strategy that only optimizes for AI Mode ignores a more engaged segment of users. I recommend optimizing for both, with different content approaches suited to each behavior pattern.

## Part 5: My Original Ideas

After reading and analyzing content from all 10 experts, I noticed one gap that none of them addressed directly. The following idea comes from my own experience as a writer and editor over 14 years, not from any source in this research.

### The Pre-AI Draft Protocol

All the experts in this research focus on how to use AI in content production, but none address the risk of AI homogenizing content voice. Tom Capper's roundup references "The Great Flattening," which describes the convergence of brand voices and language caused by AI-generated content across the internet.

My proposal: before using AI to draft any piece of content, the writer should produce a "pre-AI draft," a rough, unpolished version written entirely from their own experience and knowledge, even if it is only 200 to 300 words. This becomes the human layer that the AI draft is built around, not replaced by.

**Why it could work:** It preserves the writer's original perspective, examples, and voice. AI can expand and structure the draft, but the core insights remain human. For SEO writers with deep domain experience, particularly those with journalism backgrounds, this method protects the E-E-A-T signals (Experience, Expertise, Authoritativeness, Trustworthiness) that both Google and AI systems reward. It also directly addresses Marie Haynes' recommendation to produce content with first-hand experience and original research that AI summaries cannot replicate.

## Part 6: Weaknesses of This Playbook

Every playbook has its limits, and I think it is important to be honest about this from the start. The following weaknesses are ones I identified myself, not to undermine the content, but so readers can apply it with accurate expectations.

**1. Limited source diversity.** This playbook draws primarily from YouTube videos and LinkedIn posts, formats optimized for engagement rather than depth. The practitioners may be presenting simplified or commercially motivated versions of their actual practices.

**2. Recency bias.** All sources are from 2025 and 2026, a period of rapid change. Recommendations that are valid today, especially around GSC's Generative AI report and AI Mode behavior, may be outdated within months.

**3. No original data.** Every recommendation in this playbook is derived from secondary sources. A stronger version would include original experiments: A/B tests on content formats, personal tracking of AI citation rates, or case studies from actual content projects.

**4. Unclear applicability across industries.** Most sources focus on B2B SaaS, technology, and large-brand contexts. The recommendations may apply differently for local businesses, e-commerce, or niche content sites.

**5. The brand authority advice is difficult to operationalize.** Neil Patel notes that building recognized brand authority takes approximately 10 years. This is honest but not actionable for most content producers starting today. The playbook lacks a realistic short-term alternative for brands without existing authority.

## Part 7: Who I Would NOT Recommend Following and Why

Of the 10 experts I researched, all contributed something of value. However, after reading more closely, I found that not every perspective is equally suited to every type of practitioner. The following is my assessment of who should be approached with more caution, and why.

**Neil Patel**, with important caveats.

Neil Patel is a legitimate industry figure with real credentials and 25 or more years of experience. His YouTube video contains accurate data and useful frameworks, including the complexity moat, citation economy, and zero-click reality. These are well-argued points.

However, I would not recommend him as a primary source for working practitioners, for two reasons.

First, his framing tends toward the dramatic. Declaring that traditional SEO is effectively dead is a content strategy designed to generate engagement, not to provide nuanced guidance. The data he cites does not support such a sweeping conclusion, and it risks leading practitioners to abandon foundational SEO work that is still producing results.

Second, his advice is calibrated for large brands, not for most content producers. Building the kind of recognized brand authority he describes, with years of investment and broad publisher mentions, is not accessible to the majority of SEO writers, small teams, or early-stage B2B SaaS companies. His framework is accurate at the enterprise level but misleading when applied universally.

For practitioners at the working level, I recommend Kevin Indig for rigorous data, Matt Kenyon for practical workflows, and Lily Ray for tactical AI integrations. These three are more reliable as day-to-day guides.
